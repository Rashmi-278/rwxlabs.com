# How institutions should evaluate an Ethereum ecosystem

###### rwxLabs Research · Ecosystems · Draft
###### *Written with AI assistance from the author's prior research. To be reviewed and verified by the author before publication.*

**Audience:** Ethereum Institutional (Institutional Intelligence), Ethlabs, the EF enterprise team, and analysts comparing L1s, L2s and app-chains for institutional deployment.

**Short version:** Institutional ecosystem reports tend to swing between hype ("fastest-growing ecosystem") and single metrics (TVL, transactions). A better approach borrows established innovation-systems frameworks for the overall structure, then scores each pillar from a metric you can source and re-run, never from opinion.

---

## Start with the pillars

An ecosystem's state rests on six connected pillars:

- **Policy & regulation:** legal clarity, compliance paths, tax treatment, jurisdictional alignment.
- **Infrastructure:** client diversity, data availability, RPC and indexing, custody, oracles.
- **Talent:** active developers, education pipelines, retention of builders.
- **Market & business value:** fees, revenue, startup formation, enterprise deployments.
- **Research & innovation:** protocol R&D, standards activity, published research.
- **Societal impact:** trust, accessibility, and the CROPS properties (censorship resistance, open source, privacy, security).

## Borrow a framework, don't invent one

Economists have studied technology ecosystems for decades. Three frameworks fit Ethereum well:

| Framework | Use it to… | Ethereum example question |
|---|---|---|
| **PESTLE** | Write the executive summary of external risk | How does a jurisdiction's tax regime affect onchain activity there? |
| **Technological Innovation Systems (TIS)** | Find bottlenecks in a new technology | Is *legitimation* (countering incumbent resistance) or *resource mobilization* holding back tokenization? |
| **National Innovation Systems (NIS)** | Map how labs, firms and funders interact | How do Ethlabs, EF, Ethereum Institutional and university labs divide R&D, outreach and standards work? |

Maturity models such as the hype cycle are useful for *timing*, but they say little about *structure*. Use them last.

## Then score from data

Each pillar should get a score backed by a metric someone else can recompute. A durable ecosystem scorecard uses a small, fixed set:

1. **Real economic value:** fees users actually pay, net of incentives.
2. **Revenue drawdown:** how far activity falls from peak in a downturn.
3. **Concentration:** Herfindahl index of activity or revenue across applications. Is one app the whole ecosystem?
4. **Value accrual:** how much activity accrues to the base asset or protocol.
5. **Builder retention:** share of developers or funded projects still active a year later. Grant data helps here (see [Grant data is institutional intelligence](./01-grant-data-is-institutional-intelligence.md)).
6. **Legibility:** can an outsider get the data at all? Ecosystems with open, standardized data deserve credit for it.

Score each 0–3 with explicit thresholds, publish the inputs, and re-run quarterly. This is the same approach L2BEAT uses for rollup risk: a rubric beats a narrative because anyone can check it.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting incentivized activity as demand.** Subtract incentives before calling anything growth.
- **Comparing raw numbers across different architectures.** Normalize per user, per dollar of fees, or per developer.
- **Ignoring the input side.** Grants, hackathons and education programs are leading indicators. Revenue is a lagging one.
- **Treating CROPS as ideology.** For institutions they are risk controls; [we argue that case separately](./06-crops-for-institutions.md).
